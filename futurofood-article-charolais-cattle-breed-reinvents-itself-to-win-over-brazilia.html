Charolais cattle breed reinvents itself to win over Brazilian ranchers

Three decades of genetic selection have turned a slow-maturing giant breed into an early-finishing favorite for industrial crossbreeding in Brazil's feedlot-driven cattle industry.

For decades, Charolais cattle in Brazil carried the reputation of a large, slow-maturing breed: strong on weight gain but limited by heavier birth weights and later sexual maturity compared to the popular Angus breed, according to AgFeed.

Over the past 30 years, Brazilian breeders worked to shift that profile, importing genetics inspired by U.S. selection criteria that prioritized calving ease, polled characteristics, short coats for heat tolerance, and earlier finishing, AgFeed reports.

A pioneering ranch's four-decade bet

The transformation is rooted in Charolês Figueira, a ranch in Ararambé, Rio Grande do Sul, run by André Corrêa Berta since the 1980s. Founded in 1964 by Berta's family, the operation was among the first in Brazil to import French Charolais genetics directly, before later shifting its breeding focus toward U.S.-style selection.

"The Brazilian market discovered Charolais," Berta told AgFeed, pointing to a bull named Jotabê Patriot — weighing 867 kg — that recently surpassed 50,000 semen doses sold through CRV Lagoa, one of Brazil's largest genetic improvement centers.

Free gains from crossbreeding

Berta points to heterosis — the performance boost seen in crossbred offspring — as Charolais's key selling point in industrial crosses, calling it "the only free tool in cattle ranching" compared with costly nutrition, health, and reproduction investments, according to AgFeed.

The breed is now used both over Nelore females and, increasingly, over Angus-Nelore crossbred females in a strategy known as tricross, which combines 50% Charolais, 25% Angus, and 25% Nelore genetics to maximize growth and beef output.

Berta says feedlot results show these crosses can deliver roughly 10% higher weight gain and carcass yield than other genetic combinations, and that Charolais genetics are spreading beyond southern Brazil into states like Mato Grosso, Bahia, Sergipe, and Minas Gerais, aided by artificial insemination. He estimates Charolais semen sales could double in 2026, AgFeed reports.
